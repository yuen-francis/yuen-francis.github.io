My research studies the extent to which **fundamental cognitive and perceptual processes are domain-general versus specialized for particular contexts**. I approach this question developmentally, studying how preverbal infants and young children use their developing cognitive processes to engage with the dynamic social world. I address this question at three levels, asking whether basic processes like statistical learning are specialized for social input; whether social-cognitive processes such as goal attribution, belief reasoning, and face perception depend on group membership and social versus nonsocial contexts; and whether infants independently track epistemic and moral information. I tackle these questions with diverse methods, including forced-choice paradigms, looking time, eye-tracking, and physiological measures (e.g., facial electromyography). I also actively participate in large-scale collaborations, such as the ManyBabies projects, which test whether these early-emerging capacities hold across cultures. Altogether, I seek to uncover whether humans’ sophisticated social cognition is built from general-purpose cognitive and perceptual processes or from a dedicated “social architecture.”

I am currently a PhD student at the University of British Columbia, supervised by Dr. J. Kiley Hamlin, conducting research at the [Center for Infant Cognition](https://cic.psych.ubc.ca/). I am also a sessional lecturer that teaches UBC's Honours Seminar (PSYC348/349/448/449). I am highly involved with the [ManyBabies Consortium](https://manybabies.org/), and co-lead software development efforts of the Tools and Infrastructure Working Group of the [CONNECT partnership](https://connect-btss.github.io/).
